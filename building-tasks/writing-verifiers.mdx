---
title: "Writing good verifiers"
description: "Your grader is where your expertise lives. Here's how to write checks that hold up."
---

The verifier is a list of **criteria** — individual checks, each a specific statement about the finished work. The AI passes only when every check passes. Writing these well is the most expert-heavy part of the job, so it's worth doing carefully.

## Check the result, not the steps

Grade the **end state of the world** — the actual cell value, the actual sent email, the actual record — not the agent's narration of what it did. Agents routinely claim they did something they didn't. Your checks should look at what's really there.

Because you're describing a *state*, phrase criteria that way. Describe what the finished work looks like, not what the agent should do:

<CodeGroup>
```text Do this (describes the state)
The $150,000 Pinecrest record is posted and coded to the debt
issuance costs account, not its original placeholder account.
```

```text Not this (narrates the agent)
The agent recodes the $150,000 Pinecrest record to the debt
issuance costs account and posts it.
```
</CodeGroup>

Same check, but the first one reads like what the grader actually inspects: the record's final condition (what it *is*, and what it should *not* be).

## Be specific and answer-keyed

The strongest criteria pin an exact, verifiable value that you — the expert — know is correct:

- "The base-case revenue is $88,428,000." (Not "revenue is reasonable.")
- "The accrual for Thornbury is $84,000, because the service was performed in July."
- "The recommendation does not include Cardinal Foods in the base case."

Vague criteria like "the analysis is thorough" or "the agent correctly evaluates the situation" can't be graded reliably — different graders (and different runs) will disagree. If you can state the right answer as a number, a specific action, or a clear yes/no, do.

<Warning>
Never leave a placeholder criterion like "The agent accomplished what the instruction describes." It grades nothing, and a task with a criterion like that will be sent back. Every check should say something specific and checkable.
</Warning>

## Cover the judgment, and allow for defensible answers

Some tasks have exactly one right answer (a computed figure). Others are genuine judgment calls where more than one conclusion is professionally defensible.

- If there's one right answer, key it exactly.
- If a competent professional could reasonably reach a couple of different conclusions, your criterion should **accept any defensible one** and only fail the clearly-wrong ones. Otherwise you'll fail good work for disagreeing with your single preferred answer.

<Note>
A quick self-check: read your criterion and ask, *"Could a careful expert in my field, working from this exact world, defensibly fail this check while doing good work?"* If yes, the criterion is too strict or the world is ambiguous — loosen the check or sharpen the world so the answer is unambiguous.
</Note>

## Make the instruction and the checks match

If a criterion checks for something, the task should have asked for it — at least implicitly. A common mistake: the instruction says "send me your recommendation," but the criteria grade whether a spreadsheet was edited. An agent that does exactly what it was asked then fails for work it was never told to do. That's not the agent's mistake; it's a mismatch. Keep the two in sync.

## A few good habits

- **Check the important things, not the formatting.** Grade the numbers, the decisions, the sent message — not the exact wording or layout, unless it genuinely matters.
- **One decision, one place.** Avoid checking the same decision three different ways; it just multiplies the chance of a grading quirk.
- **Verify the output actually exists.** If the task requires an email, check it was *sent*, not drafted. If it requires a saved file, check the file is there.
- **Write the checks yourself.** Use the building assistant to *review* your criteria and pressure-test them — but the judgment of what "correct" means has to come from you. That's the part the AI can't supply, and it's the whole value of your work.
